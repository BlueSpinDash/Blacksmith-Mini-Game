How to Play

Strike every square twice. The piece on your current square decides your next move. Hot metal is unpredictable — a square can reshape itself, and a square struck once too often crumbles away.

1. Tap any square to land the first blow.
2. The chess symbol on the square you are standing on decides where the hammer may go next — the symbol on the destination does not matter.
3. Two strikes forge a square perfectly.
4. The board finishes on its own once every square has at least two strikes.

The metal reshapes. A first strike can knock a square into a different piece entirely. Above Novice this happens often enough to wreck a memorised route, so read the board again after every blow. Novice never reshapes.

Third strikes are fatal to the square. Strike a finished square again and it cracks apart. Its symbol carries you away one last time, then the square goes blank — a hole nothing can ever land on again. Blank squares still count as forged, but each one costs quality.

You can be stranded. If the square you are standing on has no legal destination left, the run is over. Spend squares carelessly and you will wall yourself in.

Endless Mode

Hit every square once to advance to the next material. The piece on your current square determines your next move. You cannot hit a square twice in the same round. Keep forging until no legal moves remain.

Endless has no difficulty to choose: every run starts on the 3×3 board and steps up a size every five rounds, up to 6×6.

Clearing a board earns a bonus and recasts it in the next metal — Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum, Mithril, Adamantine, and on into Adamantine II, III and beyond. Every round is a fresh layout with a verified route, the score carries forward, and there is no last round.

Endless scores 10 points a strike and 100 for each board cleared. Squares are struck once only, so nothing crumbles: the run ends when the square you are standing on has no unstruck square left to reach.

The metal fights back as you go. Every square can reshape under the hammer, and because you are standing on the square you just struck, a reshape redirects your very next move. The odds start low and climb by 3% every three rounds. Every five rounds the board itself steps up a tier, from 3×3 all the way to 6×6, and every symbol — king, rook, bishop, knight and queen — can turn up on any endless board.

Gold and the forge shop. Clearing a board earns gold — 1 to start, plus 0.25 more for every 300 points scored in the run, so a long run pays better and better. You are only ever paid whole coins; any leftover fraction carries over to the next board rather than being lost. It is banked the moment you earn it and kept between runs.

Spend it in the Forge Shop on the title screen. Each upgrade has ten levels: the Gilded Hammer adds a whole gold to every board, the Smith's Ledger raises your score (which in turn raises what boards pay), and Tempering cools the metal so squares reshape less often. Upgrades are permanent.

Versus Mode

You and a rival smith work separate boards, side by side — the rival on the left, you on the right. Both boards are the same size and hold the same bag of symbols, laid out differently. You strike first, then the rival, turn about.

Winning has nothing to do with points. The match ends the moment a smith has no legal square to strike. Leave the rival stranded and you win; wall yourself in and you lose.

1. Tap any square on your board to begin; after that the symbol on the square you are standing on decides where the hammer may go, exactly as in the forge.
2. Squares may be struck as often as you like here — nothing is “finished” and visiting the whole board neither wins nor resets anything.
3. A turn is at most one upgrade, then exactly one strike. You may skip the upgrade, never the strike. Concede ends the match if you would rather not play it out.

Points and the route bonus. Every strike is worth 10. Keep extending a fresh route and the multiplier climbs by 0.1 for each square in it. The symbols you pass through pay a bonus on top: Pair +5 for two of the same in a row, Three of a Kind +10 for three, Variety +8 for three all different. Only the largest pattern pays. Return to a square you have already used this route and the route restarts from that square alone, with no bonus and the multiplier back to 1.0×. Bouncing between the same two squares pays its Pair once; it will not pay again until you strike elsewhere.

The rival chases combos too. Every skill lands patterns deliberately rather than by accident, weighing what its route has already built against what one more blow could pay. It will not walk into a dead end for a combo, though — staying alive still comes first, so the surest way to spoil its run is to take its room away.

Squares darken as they are worked. A square climbs the same material ladder Endless uses — one strike Bronze, then Silver, Gold, Platinum, Mithril and Adamantine — so a glance across both boards tells you which squares each smith has been leaning on. Damage shows through whatever metal a square had reached.

Spend those points on the forge. Below the boards: Reforge (80) changes one unbroken rival square to a different symbol, Row Shuffle (60) scrambles the symbols along one rival row, Shatter (40) cracks an intact rival square, and Repair (50) clears a crack from one of your own.

Cracks, and what they cost. Cracked metal splits and glows exactly as it does in the forge on a third strike — dashed amber ring for cracked, solid red for armed. It is still perfectly safe to stand on. It only arms when struck — and it collapses into a permanent hole the moment you leave it. Repair undoes a crack, even an armed one, while you are still standing there, and the square goes straight back to the colour it was; nothing repairs a hole. So a shattered square is not a trap you must avoid, it is a trap you must pay for or walk into.

Open Your Forge

You own the shop. Buy metal, forge the stock, put it out on the shelves, price it, serve whoever walks in, and have the rent on the table at the end of every seventh day. Fall short and the landlord takes the keys.

A day is three phases — morning, afternoon, evening — and you personally do one thing in each: forge, tend the store, buy materials, stock shelves, or go looking for staff. The three phases never increase. What grows is how much gets done inside them, because employees work alongside you.

The metal decides the puzzle. Bronze needs 1 strike on every square, Silver 2, Gold 3, Mythril 4, Adamantine 5 — and one strike past that ruins the square. You may leave a square half-finished and come back to it; what you must not do is over-strike one that is already done. The forge difficulty you pick is a separate thing entirely: it sets which chess symbols appear, not how many blows a square needs.

One puzzle is one batch. Five swords take one board, not five. Finish it and the ingots are spent and the batch goes on the anvil overnight; ruin it and you lose the phase but keep the metal. How well you worked the board is the quality of the goods, and quality is what they are worth.

Nothing sells itself. Finished work lands in storage the next morning and sits there until you spend a phase carrying it out to the shelves. Every line starts at its recommended price and you may ask whatever you like — tap the price to change it. Ask too much and the piece gets hard to shift, never impossible.

Who walks in depends on what is out. Stock swords, helms and shields and you will see knights, soldiers and guards; stock daggers and boots and you will get rogues, scouts and travellers. These are leanings, not rules — anyone may buy anything. You never declare what kind of shop this is; it becomes one.

Haggling. Some customers offer under your price. Take it, refuse it, or haggle for something in between — the Haggler's Ledger and a good salesperson both improve your odds.

Staff are hands, not percentages. A Salesperson minds the counter for one phase a day, so two of them plus you keeps the shop open all day. A Runner fetches ingots, at a price that may beat the market or miss it. A Smith fills an order without you touching a board — you keep the phase, they keep the hammer, and the result is theirs. A Store Hand carries stock out for you. Only the Apprentice works beside you, making every batch you forge yourself larger. Each of the others takes one job a day, and every one of them draws a wage on rent day.

Growing costs. Bigger premises mean more staff, more shelf, more storage and bigger batches — and a much larger rent. Expanding too early is how a shop closes.

Marks: a brass diamond shows where the hammer stands now; dashed rings show every legal destination. Notches along the bottom of a square count its strikes, a red dashed border means the square is crumbling and about to blank, and a dark hole means it is already spent.

Sliding pieces jump over anything in the way — only the destination is struck. There are no captures, no blockers and no timer: this is a route-planning puzzle with a temper.
